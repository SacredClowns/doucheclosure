The James Clapper
Experience

Every other wing in this arcade runs on TOTAL BELIEF. We take the witness entirely at their word and audit only the logistics — the invoice, the cooler, the parking validation. Wing No. 15 is where that engine seizes. The Director of National Intelligence answered a yes-or-no question under oath on 12 March 2013, and about three months later the public record answered it differently. He has since described his own reply as "clearly erroneous," said he was trying to answer in what he thought was "the least untruthful manner," and said "I made a mistake but I did not lie." The board alleges nothing further. The board simply cannot process him — because he established, in his own words and on his own letterhead, that an official denial is not reliable data.

Belief Engine: Divide by Zero

Here is how the machine works everywhere else in this building. A man says he fought a crab person. We believe him. Then we ask, with total sincerity and a clipboard, where the cooler is. A man says he ran a Pentagon program. We believe him. Then we note that the Pentagon has publicly disputed the characterization, and we file the dispute next to seventeen other disputes and move on, because a denial is just one more document in the folder. That is the whole trick. Belief in, logistics out. Fourteen wings, no exceptions.

Wing No. 15 arrives at intake and the intake form catches fire. Not because the subject is a liar — the board makes no such finding, and no charges were ever brought — but because the subject is the load-bearing wall of the denial architecture itself, and he published a structural report on it. In 2013 he told the Senate Intelligence Committee "No, sir" and "Not wittingly." In the months that followed, the public learned about bulk collection of Americans' domestic call records. He then wrote to the committee's chair calling the answer "clearly erroneous," explaining that he "simply didn't think of" that program. He said elsewhere that he was trying to give the least untruthful answer to a question he compared to "when are you going to stop beating your wife."

The board wishes to stress that it is not accusing him of anything he has not already put in writing about himself. That is precisely the problem. We audit claims. He audited himself, in public, more thoroughly than we ever could, and then handed us the audit. There is nothing left on the tray.

So the belief engine hits the one input it was never built to accept. Feed it a witness: it believes and starts asking about refrigeration. Feed it a denial: it files the denial and keeps working. Feed it a denial whose author has already publicly graded it and the engine tries to believe both the statement and the correction at the same time, divides by zero, prints eleven feet of blank tape, and has to be power-cycled by an intern. Sub-Basement 4 has been rebooting it since 2013. There is a laminated sign on the housing that reads DO NOT ENTER "NOT WITTINGLY" INTO THIS TERMINAL. Somebody enters it about once a quarter. It is always somebody new.

The Eleven Words

Line itemReview board notes
THE QUESTION12 March 2013, Senate Intelligence Committee. Sen. Ron Wyden asks whether the NSA collects "any type of data at all on millions or hundreds of millions of Americans." Board's note: as questions go, this one is a closed door with one handle. There is no hallway. There is no third option. Wyden had also given advance notice that he intended to ask it, which the board mentions only because we are a logistics operation and we respect a man who books the room properly.
THE ANSWER"No, sir." Followed by: "Not wittingly. There are cases where they could, inadvertently perhaps, collect — but not wittingly." Board's finding: the initial denial is two words. The clarification is where the engineering lives.
THE ADVERBCount the clarification: Not / wittingly / There / are / cases / where / they / could / inadvertently / perhaps / collect. Eleven words. Three of them — wittingly, inadvertently, perhaps — are doing all the work, and one of them is doing more work than any adverb in the history of American governance. "Wittingly" converts a factual question about whether a thing is happening into a question about the interior mental state of an institution that has no interior. Board's ruling: the most load-bearing four syllables ever deployed in a hearing room, entered into the permanent collection with the reverence we normally reserve for a properly filled cooler.
THREE MONTHS LATERThe Snowden disclosures land, and the public record shows bulk collection of Americans' domestic call records. Board's note: the board takes no position on Snowden, who is somebody else's wing. The board notes only the interval. Ninety-odd days is a very short shelf life for a sworn denial, and our standards desk has yogurt that has outperformed it.
THE LETTERHe writes to Chair Dianne Feinstein apologizing for an answer he himself describes as "clearly erroneous," stating that he "simply didn't think of" the call-records program. Elsewhere he says he was trying to respond in "the least untruthful manner" to what he likened to a "when are you going to stop beating your wife" question, and separately that "I made a mistake but I did not lie." No charges were brought. Board's note, filed without snark: this is the entire evidentiary basis of this wing. We did not have to dig. We did not have to speculate. Every document in this table is his, or the committee's, or the public record's. That is the standard we hold every other wing to and it is nice, for once, to be handed it.
THE ACCIDENTAL GIFTFor seventy years, the standard closing argument against an experiencer was four words long: "the government says no." Every rural sheriff, every editor, every brother-in-law at Thanksgiving had it in the holster, and it always worked, because an official denial was treated as terminal. Then a sitting Director of National Intelligence stood up and demonstrated — under oath, and then again, more thoroughly, in his own apology letter — that an official denial is a statement made by a person, subject to the same review as any other statement. Board's finding: every single person who was ever waved off with "the government says no" now has a citation. He did not mean to give them one. He gave them one anyway. It is the most valuable object anyone has ever left in this building by accident.

The Award He Will Not Accept

For decades the conversation ended the same way. Somebody described what they saw. Somebody else said "well, the government says no," and that was the end of the sentence, the end of the report, and quite often the end of the friendship. There was no counterargument available, because the denial was treated as the floor of reality rather than a document produced by a person having a Tuesday.

Then, in a hearing room in March 2013, an official gave an eleven-word clarification he would later describe himself as "clearly erroneous," and the floor turned out to be a rug. He did not intend that. He has said plainly that he made a mistake, that he simply didn't think of the program, and that he did not lie, and the board takes him at his word exactly as it takes every witness in this building at theirs — which is completely.

But intent is not the finding. The finding is what he handed people. Every experiencer who was ever dismissed with an official denial now has a citation, from the Director of National Intelligence, that an official denial can be wrong and can be corrected and is not the end of any sentence. Nobody had that before. Everybody has it now. He is, by acclamation of this board and entirely against his own will, the most effective disclosure advocate in American history.

The board voted to give him an award. He will not accept it. It has been sent back three times. It currently sits in Sub-Basement 4, next to the belief engine, on a shelf of its own, with his name engraved on it and no plans to move. The plaque is short. It reads: NOT WITTINGLY. He earned every word of that, and the board means it sincerely, and the board would like the record to show it is the only award in this building that was won by telling the truth about a mistake.
